Guide

Why this handbook is built the way it is, and how to make it work for you.

1Design idea: Meaning Over Words.

Chinese speakers start learning idioms (成語, chéngyǔ) in kindergarten; they are a language asset rooted deep in memory. Yet the moment English enters the picture, many people forget that asset and try to carry the characters across one by one. The result is either stiff or simply off.

This site starts from one simple idea: don’t let the literal wording of an idiom hold you hostage. What we do instead is wake up the conversion knowledge already in your head and find, for each idiom, the English expression that is “functionally equivalent”: whatever role the idiom plays in the sentence and whatever tone it carries, the English takes on the same job.

The point is not to look alike, but to work alike.

So you will see some idioms matched with a genuine English idiom and others with just an ordinary word. Both are good answers, as long as they say the same thing on that occasion.

2Theory: grasp the meaning, convey the sense

This handbook is not paired up by gut feeling; it rests on interpreting and translation theory. At its core are two closely linked ideas.

得意忘形 (dé yì wàng xíng): grasp the meaning, forget the form

The idiom 得意忘形 originally describes someone so pleased with himself that he loses all composure. Translators and interpreters borrow it and read it the other way round: grasp the meaning (意) and forget the form (形).

For an interpreter, that means catching the core message and spirit behind the speaker’s words (the meaning), letting go of the literal structure and grammar of the original (the form), and rebuilding it naturally in the target language.

  • Capture the meaning rather than the form.重意不重形
  • Translate the sense, not the words.譯意不譯字
  • Focus on the core message, not the literal wording.聚焦核心訊息，而非字面措辭

傳意不傳詞 (chuán yì bù chuán cí): the core rule of modern interpreting

“Convey the sense, not the words” (also 譯意不譯詞) stresses that the interpreter conveys the speaker’s real intent and tone instead of mechanically matching word for word.

DeverbalizationFirst turn what you hear into a pure concept or image, then say it again following the natural logic of the target language.
Keep the pragmatics and the feelingImplication, cultural background, humor and emotion all have to come across, so that the audience reacts the way the original audience did.
Bridge the differences in grammarLanguages order and package things differently; insisting on “the words” only produces stiff translationese.

Three examples show the difference:

Let’s not boil the ocean here.

Words ✗我們不要在這裡把海洋煮沸。“Let’s not bring the ocean to a boil here.”

Sense ✓我們別把事情搞得太複雜，先聚焦重點吧。“Let’s not overcomplicate this; let’s focus on what matters first.”

Break a leg!

Words ✗打斷一條腿！“Snap a leg in two!”

Sense ✓祝你演出順利！“Hope the show goes well!”

It is not impossible that he will come.

Words ✗他會來這件事不是不可能的。The double negative, carried over intact, reads awkwardly in Chinese.

Sense ✓他還是有可能會來的。“He may still come.”

“Conveying the sense” does not mean improvising. Proper nouns, legal provisions, contract figures and technical specifications must be rendered strictly word for word so the information stays accurate. And when a speaker deliberately chooses vague or forceful wording (in diplomacy, for example), the same tone should be kept, without polishing or softening it on your own.

Idioms are the best practice ground for this idea: behind every idiom is a meaning you already know well. What this handbook does is help you step around the literal form and go straight to the functionally equivalent English.

3The One–Four Rule: one word, four characters

The One–Four Rule (一四法則) exists to avoid translationese and keep expression idiomatic, elegant and vivid. Chinese likes parallelism, reduplication and four-character phrases for emphasis and rhythm; English has no such habit, and translating every character only adds bulk. So each direction makes its own trade-off: English into Chinese can expand; Chinese into English should condense.

Applied to idioms, this handbook follows one simple rule: one English word can call to mind a four-character Chinese idiom, and a four-character idiom can flexibly become one English word or a simple phrase.

EN → ZH · Expand: four characters is ideal

One English word calls up a four-character idiom

There is no need to match the word count. Rendering the word vividly with a four-character idiom is a chance to show classical literacy, shift parts of speech and set a mood.

  1. First turn the English word (noun, verb, adjective or adverb) into plain Chinese.
  2. Draw on your Chinese to extend it into four characters or a full phrase.
  3. Four characters hang together and roll off the tongue, so saying them straight out usually works.
  • solemn→莊嚴→莊嚴肅穆zhuāng yán sù mù
  • my pleasure→很樂意→樂意之至lè yì zhī zhì
  • outstanding→傑出→出類拔萃chū lèi bá cuì

Upside: concise and rhythmic, understood in a single line, and it saves time.

ZH → EN · Condense: one word will do

A four-character idiom becomes one word or a simple phrase

Keep it lean: convey the meaning, don’t copy the meter. No character-by-character matching is needed; one word or a simple phrase can carry all four characters.

  1. When you meet an idiom, first think of its plain-language core meaning.
  2. From that core meaning, look for related English: a word, a phrase or a short sentence all work.
  3. English is strict about grammar and word pairing, so watch collocations, occasion and register.
  • 牢不可破láo bù kě pò→ironclad
  • 津津有味jīn jīn yǒu wèi→with relish
  • 畫蛇添足huà shé tiān zú→overdo it

Watch out: never translate character by character, or you end up with stiff Chinglish. Keep an eye out, too, for expressions with a cultural echo: Western customs, the Bible, Greece and Rome, Shakespeare, and their present-day counterparts.

  • Register and over-translation: In both directions, mind the register and don’t read more into the original than is there. Beginners can simply switch to plain language first, then polish rhyme and matching length when they have capacity to spare.
  • When to use it: Judge by the situation. When the interpreting job or the text does not suit ornate wording, don’t stall or lag just to squeeze in an idiom; where emphasis or polish fits, bring one in naturally.
  • How to practice: Make a habit of turning everyday two-character words into four-character idioms; memorize more idioms, build associations, and make good use of shifts in part of speech. In Taiwan most interpreting runs from English into Chinese, so this drill gets a lot of use.

“One–Four” is a memory aid, not a hard word-count rule: the point is that each language takes its most natural, most economical wording.

4How to learn: learn the usage, not just the word

Memorizing idioms the way you memorize vocabulary is a perfectly good thing to do. It is the most solid place to start, and there is no need to feel it is unsophisticated.

The translator’s aim goes one step further, though: the idiom is already in your head, and now you only need to activate its functional equivalence with English. That is why every idiom comes with an English example sentence showing how the expression sits in a real sentence, and why “Study mode” can hide the English or the Chinese, letting you make the switch in your head before you reveal the answer.

If the idioms themselves are unfamiliar, this site is a chance to strengthen your Chinese while picking up the English. Even if you do not work with languages, a basic feel for language is a good way to sharpen your competitive edge.

In this English edition, every idiom also carries Hanyu Pinyin with tone marks, so readers whose first language is not Chinese can read it aloud.

    7Reading notes

    This site collects more than traditional chengyu: it also includes four-character expressions that are common in everyday spoken Chinese.

    English equivalents follow the principle of “same meaning”: some are idioms, some are just ordinary words. The tone and formality of an idiom and its English may not match exactly; for teaching, writing and interpreting, choose by context and double-check where it matters.

    The example sentences were written to show usage; feel free to rework them to fit your own situation. Icons, pinyin and the pinyin index are compiled with automated help, so a few slips are inevitable; if you spot an error or know a better rendering, corrections are welcome.

    Pinyin follows standard Hanyu Pinyin and is written without tone changes (不 is always bù, 一 always yī). A few characters are commonly read differently in Taiwan.

    About this site

    A Bridge Between Languages.

    ECHO grew out of the author’s personal idiom word bank, compiled over many years. As a teacher and language professional, the author hopes to help strengthen Chinese-language fundamentals and foreign-language skills among the general public. Its aim is to give students, teachers and language professionals a Chinese–English idiom resource organized by occasion, with English equivalents and example sentences.

    The site is updated from time to time: chapters and entries are expanded or revised to take in new words and expressions the author comes across. Planned updates include expressions for more situations and new features. This English edition is meant for readers whose first language is not Chinese, and further language editions are being considered to serve international readers. For feedback or teaching collaborations, you are welcome to write; contact details are on the author’s interpreter website.
