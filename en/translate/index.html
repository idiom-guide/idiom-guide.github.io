Translationese Checker

When an English-to-Chinese translation copies English sentence structure, the Chinese reads as “translationese.” When a Chinese-to-English translation copies Chinese phrasing, the English turns wordy and stiff. Internet slang suffers from the same faults. This column collects the common problems with rewrites, plus an online checker and a pre-delivery checklist.

English → Chinese: 12 types of translationese

The classification follows 歐冠宇 (2020), who interviewed twelve Taiwanese academics and practitioners and grouped translationese into 12 types, arranged here in four groups. Open each type to see the English source sentence, the translationese Chinese and a rewrite, with a comment on what went wrong. The examples are Chinese sentences and are shown in the original.

Fixing translationese does not mean chopping sentences short. Legal provisions and academic arguments are complex by nature, so long sentences are unavoidable. The aim is a long sentence with a clear structure: cut padding, keep subject and verb close together, and state the condition before the result. Tolerance for translationese also varies by era and region; some expressions that once sounded like translationese are now natural Chinese and need not be rejected.
SEVEN CORRECTION TECHNIQUES

Chinese → English: writing concise English

English prefers the simple, concrete and direct. Chinese habits such as 進行, 加以 and 就……而言, copied into English, turn into stacked nouns and padding. The items below draw on two translation-writing guides from the European Commission, plus common Chinglish among Taiwanese learners. Each line starts from the Chinese phrase that triggers the problem.

Translationese in internet slang

Some of these expressions are calques from English; some spread first through mainland Chinese social networks and reached Taiwan through short-video platforms; others are home-grown in Taiwan, such as the “language cancer” (語言癌) debate of 2014. The overuse of 作為 and 被 is in fact much older: the poet and essayist Yu Kwang-chung (余光中) criticized this kind of “vicious Westernization” as early as 1979. Slang is harmless in chat, but in homework, official documents or translations, natural Chinese is clearer. Examples that depend on Chinese-only wordplay cannot be carried over into English; they are kept in the original and marked.
