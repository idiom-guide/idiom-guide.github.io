AI Can Write the Essay, but It Can't Speak for You

A student hands in a polished, well-structured English essay. Can you be sure it reflects what that student can actually do? Over the past two years, nearly every English teacher has asked this question. My view: an essay can be ghostwritten by AI, but nobody can speak on a student’s behalf. That is only half right, though.

A beautiful essay proves very little

A well-written essay does not mean the author has the skill behind it. Real ability shows within a few minutes of conversation. AI can write for a student, but it cannot sit in the interview room and answer for them.

Volume, rhythm, intonation, eye contact and the ability to think on your feet when a follow-up question comes: all of these ultimately have to be trained in the person who will be speaking.

But AI can already listen

The claim that speaking is out of AI’s reach needs a discount. Many tools can now analyze a student’s recording and report on speaking rate, pauses, filler words and pronunciation. Voice modes can also simulate interview questions, so a student can rehearse alone, again and again.

AI is not all-powerful, of course. Its reading of intonation and stress is still crude. Whether a talk is persuasive, whether it moves the audience, how well a speaker interacts with the people in the room: humans still judge these better.

What AI cannot do is speak for you or perform in front of real people. Spotting what is wrong with your speaking, it can already help with.

That suggests a practical division of labor. Students record themselves first and ask AI to flag speaking rate, filler words and pronunciation problems. In class, the teacher coaches and AI plays the practice partner.

Three things teaching needs to change

  1. Assessment. A finished product no longer proves ability, so give more weight to in-class writing, oral exams and recordings paired with reflection. Interpreter training, which centers on live delivery, offers plenty of methods worth borrowing.
  2. The teacher’s role. The center of gravity moves toward coach and gatekeeper: deciding what to teach, what good looks like, where a student is stuck, and how to get them willing to speak.
  3. AI literacy. Students need to learn this too: how to ask, how to catch AI’s mistakes, and when not to use it.

What to hand to AI, and what to keep

Fine to hand to AI

  • Writing questions: varied questions, cloze tests, reading questions and answer explanations. Always check the answers yourself, because AI makes occasional mistakes.
  • First-pass essay feedback: let AI look at grammar, word choice and structure first, so the teacher can focus on the main points and decide the grade.
  • Summarizing the class’s common errors, designing classroom activities, and drafting announcements and emails.

Still needs a person

  • Final grading, and responsibility for appeals
  • Modeling speech and giving live feedback
  • Knowing each student and sparking motivation
  • Confirming that “you really wrote this”

In short

AI has made producing text fast and cheap, which makes real performance in the moment worth more. Being able to speak, to think on your feet and to keep practicing is the English a student can truly take away. The teacher’s job is to make the classroom the place where those abilities happen.
